Curiosity got me started. The internet, and later AI, showed me I could actually build.

I grew up in Rui, a small village near Ichalkaranji in Kolhapur. My father is an accountant, and the first computer I ever touched was his: Microsoft tools, websites, and, when he wasn't looking, games. I didn't understand how any of it worked. I wanted to.

I left home for a hostel in 4th standard. In 5th, I was selected for both Satara Military School and PM SHRI Jawahar Navodaya Vidyalaya. I chose Navodaya. It was central, it was CBSE, and honestly, I was always more of a maths kid than a parade-ground kid.

Navodaya had a computer lab full of PCs. Getting a seat was the hard part. I spent a good part of school trying to be the kid who got one.

In 10th, I scored 94.6% and became school captain. That year was golden. The maths kid who skipped military school ended up as main parade commander for Independence Day. I organised sports events and programs, and learned more about leadership, management, and people than any textbook taught me.

I was still miles away from code.

In 11th, the school gave us tablets. That's where I met ChatGPT. I kept asking it questions, then started asking how it could answer them at all. That question led me to LLMs, and LLMs led me to code.

After 12th, I took a drop year for JEE. Coding had other plans. I started learning Python and how technology actually works underneath. JEE pulled me back, and the learning stalled. I picked it up again the week I walked into college.

I chose Mechatronics because I'm curious about everything. I was going to learn software anyway, but I wanted to understand hardware too: mechanical, electrical, all of it. I want to be someone who knows software and understands every part of engineering.

In my first week of college, Smart India Hackathon came around. Our idea was [Career Copilot](/projects/career-copilot), and we needed a website to present it. I had never built one. So I skipped the tutorials and started building, asking Gemini for guidance one step at a time and learning GitHub and VS Code along the way. Then I found Antigravity and Claude, and something clicked.

We cleared the college's internal rounds. We didn't go further, but it was the push I needed.

I joined three clubs as a volunteer: the Mechatronics Students Association, [Team Gladiator](/projects/gladiator-racing) (our ATV team), and the [Innovation and Startup Cell](/projects/innovation-and-startup-cell). Within a year, I became head of the powertrain team at Gladiator and head of web design and development at the Innovation and Startup Cell. Neither had a website before. Now both do.

I'm co-founding **[CounselPro](/projects/counselpro)**, college counselling for the JEE, NEET, and CET students I was not long ago.

Everything else I build comes back to one question: what if AI did the work, and still asked before it acted?

**[STEVE](/projects/steve)** is for the solo founder who has to be eight people at once: engineer, marketer, salesperson, designer, support, ops, finance and legal. It gives each of those jobs an always-on AI department that does the real work, from opening pull requests to deploying sites. Anything that emails a customer or spends money waits for the founder's approval.

**[Rocky](/projects/rocky)** is my memory. It remembers my meetings, lectures, messages and documents, runs on my own machine, and quotes the exact passage behind every answer. When nothing backs an answer, it says "Not found in your sources" instead of guessing.

**[DStack](/projects/dstack)** gives building with AI a shape. It turns a rough idea into a pipeline of planning, review, QA and shipping, spread across 42 slash commands. `/ship` stays locked until review and QA pass.

Three products, one rule: the AI does the work, and a human gets the final say.

I'm still the curious kid from Rui. I just have better tools now.
